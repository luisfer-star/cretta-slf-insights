How to Prepare a LATAM Company
for US Capital and
Board Standards

The distance between a well-run LATAM company and a US-capital-ready LATAM company is not primarily a distance of quality. It is a distance of language, architecture, and institutional credibility — and it is navigable, but only if you understand exactly what you are navigating toward.

By Luis Fernando Jiménez M.
Category Cross-Border Capital · LATAM
Read ~12 min

Latin American companies that have built genuine businesses — real revenues, real customers, real competitive positions — frequently discover that the path to US institutional capital is longer and more demanding than they anticipated. Not because their businesses are not good. Because the institutional framework within which US capital allocators evaluate investment opportunities is built on assumptions, standards, and communication norms that are not the natural operating environment of most LATAM companies. The gap is real. And it is closable — but only by companies that understand exactly what they are closing.

I — Understanding the Gap

What US capital actually evaluates — and why the LATAM default falls short

US institutional capital — private equity, family offices, growth equity funds, strategic investors — evaluates LATAM companies through a framework that combines financial analysis with institutional quality assessment. The financial analysis is familiar: revenue growth, margins, unit economics, market size, competitive position. The institutional quality assessment is less familiar, less articulated, and often more decisive than the financial analysis when both are adequate.

Institutional quality assessment asks whether the company operates in a way that US capital can trust — with the governance, the financial reporting, the management depth, and the strategic communication sophistication that allows a US investor to understand, monitor, and protect their investment from the other side of a significant cultural and geographic distance. A company that fails this assessment — regardless of the quality of its financial performance — will either not attract US capital or will attract it on terms that reflect the perceived institutional risk.

The challenge for most LATAM companies is that their institutional infrastructure was built for their home market environment — which has different standards for financial reporting, different governance norms, different management culture, and different expectations for investor communication than the US institutional context. This is not a deficiency — it is an adaptation to a different environment. But it creates a gap that must be closed before US capital is accessible on reasonable terms.

US investors do not invest in businesses they cannot understand. The LATAM company that cannot communicate its business in the language, format, and institutional framework that US capital expects will not close an investment regardless of the quality of its underlying business. The translation work is not optional.

II — The Readiness Domains

The five domains where LATAM companies must build readiness — and what each requires

Readiness for US capital is not a single condition — it is a state of adequacy across five distinct domains, each of which US investors evaluate independently and each of which can disqualify an investment if it falls below the minimum threshold. The domains must be developed in parallel — deficiency in any one of them will surface during due diligence and affect either the probability of closing or the terms of the investment.

01
Financial reporting and controls

US institutional investors require financial statements that they can trust — which means statements audited by a firm they recognize, presented in a format they can interpret, and supported by internal controls they can evaluate. For most LATAM companies, this requires a shift from the statutory accounting that serves local regulatory purposes to a management accounting framework that serves investor decision-making purposes.

The specific requirements vary by investor type: private equity firms typically require IFRS or US GAAP with an audit by a recognized firm; growth equity investors may accept local GAAP with a quality of earnings analysis by a reputable firm. The minimum that virtually all US institutional investors require is three years of audited financial statements — which means the preparation for US capital must begin at least three years before the targeted investment closing date.

Audited financials (recognized auditor), management accounts, clean cap table, documented related-party transactions, CFO with investor-grade reporting capability
02
Corporate governance and legal structure

US investors invest in companies whose governance they can understand and whose legal structure allows them to hold their investment in a manner that is legally protected and practically enforceable. For LATAM companies with complex holding structures, related-party arrangements, or informal governance, this requires significant structural cleanup before the investment process begins.

The governance work has two components: the structural (the legal entities, the ownership documentation, the shareholder agreements, the Board composition and charter) and the functional (whether the Board actually governs, whether management is actually accountable to the Board, whether investor rights are actually protected). Structural governance without functional governance is visible to experienced US investors and reduces investment confidence regardless of how clean the documentation appears.

Clean legal structure, documented shareholder rights, functioning Board with independent directors, investment agreement that meets US market standards
03
Management team depth and capability

The management team that US investors evaluate is not the founder — it is the team that will run the business after the investment, potentially without the founder's daily involvement. US investors want to see functional depth: a CFO who can manage investor reporting, a commercial leader who can articulate the go-to-market strategy with data, an operations leader who can explain the unit economics, and a technology or product leader (depending on the business) who can speak to the competitive differentiation.

Key-person concentration — where the business depends on one or two individuals who cannot be replaced — is one of the most consistent risk factors US investors identify in LATAM companies. The solution is not a succession planning document — it is demonstrably developing and promoting the next generation of leadership before the investor process begins, so that the team's depth is visible in the track record, not just claimed in the narrative.

Functional depth at C-suite level, management team that has operated together through a full business cycle, demonstrable succession depth below the founder level
04
Strategic narrative and investor communication

The investment thesis — the explicit, data-supported argument for why this company will create significant value over the investment horizon — must be communicated in the format and language that US investors use to evaluate it. This is not the story the founder tells about the company's history and vision. It is a structured argument about market size, competitive position, business model economics, growth vectors, and risk factors that can be evaluated by someone who has never met the founder and knows nothing about the specific market.

Most LATAM company founders find this format initially unnatural — because they are accustomed to building investor confidence through relationship, through demonstration, and through the personal trust that comes from sustained interaction. US institutional investors, operating at scale across many opportunities, require the thesis to stand on its own before they will invest the relationship capital that personal interaction requires. The investment memo that does not survive a cold read by a partner who has not met the company will not produce a term sheet regardless of how compelling the founder relationship is.

Data-supported investment thesis, structured management presentation, financial model with scenarios, clear use of proceeds, defined exit paths
05
Compliance, legal cleanliness, and institutional credibility

US institutional investors conduct thorough legal due diligence on LATAM companies — not because they assume something is wrong, but because the legal and compliance landscape in LATAM is complex and the consequences of undisclosed issues for a US investor are significant. Related-party transactions, employment informality, environmental compliance, regulatory status, and beneficial ownership transparency are the areas where LATAM companies most frequently have issues that require resolution before US capital can close.

The principle that applies is disclosure and remediation, not concealment. Issues that are disclosed early and remediated credibly rarely disqualify a company. Issues that surface during due diligence after not being disclosed frequently do — because they raise questions about what else was not disclosed. The legal preparation for US capital should begin two to three years before the investment process — not two to three months before, when there is no time to remediate what the lawyers find.

Clean legal due diligence, FCPA/anti-corruption compliance, disclosed and documented related parties, environmental compliance, beneficial ownership transparency
III — The Preparation Sequence

A three-phase preparation framework — timed against the investment process

The preparation for US capital is a multi-year process that must be sequenced against the investment timeline. The following three-phase framework organizes the preparation work in the order that produces the best outcome — building foundational infrastructure before developing investor-facing materials, and developing institutional relationships before entering a formal investment process.

Three-phase preparation framework — 24–36 months before investment close
Phase I — Months 1–12
Foundation — build what cannot be rushed
  • Engage recognized audit firm and begin restated financials
  • Clean legal structure and document ownership fully
  • Identify and remediate related-party transactions
  • Formalize employment arrangements and compliance
  • Appoint CFO with investor-grade capability
  • Begin Board professionalization — add first independent director
Horizon: 3 years of clean audited financials by investment close
Phase II — Months 12–24
Architecture — build investor-ready systems
  • Develop management reporting framework with investor-grade KPIs
  • Build financial model with scenario analysis
  • Develop management team depth — visible succession below founder
  • Establish investor communication rhythm — quarterly reporting discipline
  • Complete Board governance — add second independent director
  • Begin US market relationship development
Horizon: institutional infrastructure visible and demonstrably functional
Phase III — Months 24–36
Positioning — enter market with credibility
  • Finalize investment thesis and management presentation
  • Engage investment banker or placement agent with LATAM-US mandate
  • Conduct structured investor introduction process
  • Begin formal due diligence with qualified investors
  • Negotiate term sheet with investor-grade advisors
  • Close investment on terms that reflect institutional quality
Horizon: close investment on terms reflecting the institutional quality built in Phases I–II
IV — The Common Mistakes

Five ways LATAM companies compromise their US capital process

The preparation mistakes that most frequently compromise the US capital process for LATAM companies are not random — they follow recognizable patterns. Each can be avoided with the right preparation architecture and the right advisory team.

01
Starting the investor process before the institutional infrastructure is ready
The most common and most costly mistake: approaching US investors before the financial reporting, governance, and legal cleanliness that US due diligence requires are in place. The investor process reveals the deficiencies rather than the company choosing when and how to remediate them. Once an investor has identified a significant due diligence issue, the negotiating position is permanently weakened — even if the issue is subsequently resolved. The remediation that should have occurred before the process changes the investor's perception of the company for the entire remaining process.
02
Treating the investment narrative as a translation exercise
Taking the narrative the company uses for LATAM investors and translating it into English does not produce a US-investor-ready narrative. The narrative must be rebuilt from the investor's perspective — starting from the investment thesis, not from the company's history. The US investor is not interested in the founder's journey — they are interested in the thesis for why this company will produce the return they require. These are not the same narrative, and confusing them produces presentations that feel authentic to the founder and inadequate to the investor.
03
Engaging the wrong advisors for the cross-border process
The legal, financial, and strategic advisors who serve the company well in the LATAM context are not necessarily the right team for the US capital process. US institutional investors have preferences about the caliber of advisors they work with — and a company represented by advisors that US investors do not recognize or respect starts the process at a disadvantage. The advisory team for a US capital raise should be selected for their US institutional credibility, not for their existing relationship with the company founder.
04
Underestimating the time and management bandwidth the process requires
A well-run US institutional investment process — from initial investor introductions to closing — typically takes 9–18 months and consumes a significant portion of the CEO and CFO's bandwidth during that period. Companies that enter the process without this understanding consistently find that the investment process compromises operational performance — which is precisely visible to investors during the same period. The management team that allows the investment process to degrade their business performance is providing evidence during the investment process of the key-person concentration risk that investors were already concerned about.
05
Accepting terms that reflect preparation deficiencies rather than business quality
Companies that enter the US capital process with deficiencies in their institutional infrastructure frequently close investments — but on terms that reflect those deficiencies: higher dilution, stronger investor control provisions, more onerous reporting requirements, and more aggressive ratchet mechanisms. These terms are the market's way of pricing the institutional risk that the company's preparation did not address. The investment that closes on unfavorable terms because the company was not adequately prepared costs more over the investment horizon than the preparation would have cost — by a large margin.

The preparation that earns institutional credibility

US capital is available to LATAM companies that have built genuine businesses. The companies that access it on the best terms are the ones that have invested in the institutional infrastructure that US capital requires — not because US capital is better than LATAM capital, but because the institutional standards that US capital demands are genuinely valuable for the company's own governance and strategic clarity, independently of any capital transaction.

The LATAM company that builds investor-grade financial reporting, functional governance, management depth, and a rigorous strategic narrative is not just preparing for US capital. It is building the institutional quality that makes the business more valuable, more resilient, and more capable of attracting and deploying capital of any origin more effectively.

The preparation is not a concession to US capital's preferences — it is an investment in the company's own institutional quality. The capital access it enables is the return on that investment.
