What Real Accountability
Looks Like in a
Strategic Mandate

Accountability is the most used — and most abused — word in the strategic advisory vocabulary. Almost every engagement includes it. Very few are structured to produce it. Understanding the difference is the most important due diligence a client can do before engaging any strategic leadership actor.

By Luis Fernando Jiménez M.
Category Strategic Mandate
Read ~11 min

Every strategic advisor claims accountability. The word appears in every engagement letter, every pitch deck, every introductory conversation. And yet the most common complaint from clients who have worked with strategic advisors — consultancies, fractional executives, advisory boards — is precisely the absence of accountability for outcomes. The recommendation was delivered. The work was done. The result was not produced. And the advisor, having fulfilled their contractual obligation to the process rather than to the outcome, moved on. Real accountability in a strategic mandate looks structurally different from this. And the structure is visible before the engagement begins.

I — The Accountability Problem

Why claimed accountability rarely produces real accountability

The accountability problem in strategic advisory is not a character problem — it is a structural problem. It arises from a fundamental misalignment between what advisors are contracted to deliver (a process, a deliverable, a set of recommendations) and what clients actually need (a result). When the contract is for the process and the result does not come, the advisor has technically honored their commitment. The client has technically received what they contracted for. And both parties are left with an outcome that serves neither.

This misalignment persists for three reasons. First, outcomes in high-complexity strategic situations are genuinely difficult to specify in advance — they depend on variables that neither party controls, on market dynamics that shift during execution, and on the decisions of third parties who are not at the table. Second, advisors who commit to outcomes carry a fundamentally different risk profile than those who commit to process — and the compensation structures required to align those risks are more complex and less familiar than hourly retainers. Third, clients often resist outcome-based structures because they imply greater shared accountability on their side — the access, trust, and tolerance for process that make outcome delivery possible are conditions the client must provide.

The result is a market that converges on process accountability as the default — because it is familiar, legally clean, and comfortable for both parties — and that consistently underdelivers on the outcomes that make strategic engagement valuable in the first place. Real accountability requires a different structure. It begins with a different kind of contract — not a legal document, but a relational architecture that aligns every element of the engagement toward the result.

The advisor who claims accountability without outcome-linked compensation has defined accountability as something they cannot actually lose. That is not accountability — it is the vocabulary of accountability applied to a structure that makes it impossible.

II — The Accountability Spectrum

Four levels of accountability — where most engagements fall, and where they should

Accountability in strategic advisory exists on a spectrum. Most engagements cluster at the low end. Understanding the full spectrum is the prerequisite for recognizing where a given engagement falls — and for demanding the level that the stakes require.

The accountability spectrum — from process to outcome
Level 1
Process accountability
Responsible for doing the work: attending the sessions, producing the deliverables, fulfilling the scope. The most common level. No accountability for whether the work produces results. Termination risk: zero, if the process is followed.
Level 2
Recommendation accountability
Responsible for the quality of the analysis and the soundness of the recommendations — which the client must then implement. One step beyond process, but still insulated from execution. Termination risk: low, if the recommendations are defensible.
Level 3 — Target
Implementation accountability
Responsible for the quality of implementation as well as the recommendation. The advisor does not step back when the recommendation is delivered — they co-lead the implementation. Compensation partially tied to implementation milestones.
Level 4
Outcome accountability
Responsible for the result. Compensation is structurally tied to whether the mandate produces the agreed outcome. The advisor carries genuine risk if the outcome is not produced — and genuine upside if it is. The defining level of a Strategic Leadership Mandate.

Most strategic advisory engagements operate at Level 1 or Level 2. A small number operate at Level 3. The Strategic Leadership Mandate, as Cretta defines and structures it, operates at Level 4 — with meaningful variable compensation tied to the outcome, not to the process or the deliverable. The level of accountability is not determined by what the advisor says — it is determined by what they stand to lose if the outcome is not produced.

III — The Contrast

Fake accountability vs. real accountability — what each looks like in practice

The distinction between claimed and genuine accountability is visible in the specific behaviors each produces — before, during, and after the critical moments of a strategic mandate. The following comparisons are not theoretical — they are patterns drawn from the behavioral difference between engagements structured for process accountability and those structured for outcome accountability.

IV — The Requirements

What real accountability in a strategic mandate structurally requires

Real accountability is not a posture — it is an architecture. It requires specific structural elements on both sides of the engagement. The following five elements define what that architecture looks like from the advisor's side.

01
A clearly defined result — not a process
The mandate must specify what success looks like in concrete, observable terms — not in terms of activities, deliverables, or effort. "Close the transaction at or above the target valuation" is a result. "Provide M&A advisory services" is a process. The accountability structure can only be as strong as the clarity of the result it is accountable to. Vague results produce vague accountability — regardless of how strongly worded the language of commitment is.
02
Variable compensation with genuine downside — not just upside
The compensation structure that produces real accountability is one where the advisor bears a genuine financial consequence if the result is not produced — not just foregoes a bonus. A structure that offers upside if the result is delivered but has no downside if it is not is not outcome accountability — it is a bonus structure for good performance. Real accountability requires that the advisor's financial position is genuinely affected by the outcome in both directions.
03
Executive authority within the mandate's scope
An actor who is accountable for a result must have the authority to pursue it. An advisor who is accountable for the outcome of a transaction but cannot speak on behalf of the client in negotiations, cannot direct the attorney and the financial advisor, and cannot make decisions within defined parameters without escalating every step — is accountable for a result they cannot pursue effectively. Accountability without authority is responsibility without power — and it produces exactly the frustrating dynamic that accountability-washing creates.
04
Full and continuous information access
The actor accountable for the result must have access to the information required to pursue it — not filtered access, not periodic updates, not the information management chooses to share. Strategic mandates in high-complexity environments require real-time situational awareness. An advisor who discovers critical information late — because it was not shared, or because the information system was not designed for their access level — cannot bear genuine accountability for what their partial information led them to recommend. Information asymmetry between the client and the mandate actor is structurally incompatible with genuine outcome accountability.
05
Continuity through the full mandate lifecycle
A mandate that terminates at the first sign of difficulty is not a mandate — it is a project with an accountability vocabulary. Real accountability requires that the engagement continues as long as the result is achievable — not as long as the conditions are comfortable. The actor's commitment to the result must be stronger than their preference for a comfortable process. This is the element that most clearly distinguishes genuine accountability from its imitation: the willingness to stay when the situation makes staying difficult.
V — The Client's Side

What real accountability requires from the client — not just the advisor

Real accountability in a strategic mandate is bilateral. The five structural requirements described in the previous section define what the advisor must provide. But an advisor who provides all five cannot produce genuine accountability without a corresponding set of conditions from the client. These conditions are not preferences — they are prerequisites. Without them, the mandate structure collapses regardless of how strong the advisor's commitment is.

What the client must provide for real accountability to function
Deep bilateral trust — before the engagement begins
An advisor with outcome accountability will say difficult things, hold positions under pressure, and make decisions the client may not initially agree with. This requires a level of trust that cannot be built during the engagement — it must exist before it begins. A client who does not trust the advisor's judgment cannot grant the access and authority that genuine accountability requires.
Full and unfiltered information access
The client must provide the mandate actor with the same quality of information they would use to make the decision themselves — including information that reflects poorly on the client's current situation, on past decisions, or on the constraints the client would prefer not to have to acknowledge. A client who filters the information controls the accountability — and makes genuine outcome commitment impossible.
Tolerance for the process — including its difficult moments
High-complexity strategic mandates always pass through difficult moments — moments where the outcome is uncertain, the path is unclear, and the pressure to change direction or reduce the scope is high. A client who responds to those moments by renegotiating the mandate, pulling back the access, or reducing the authority undermines the accountability architecture they contracted for.
The willingness to accept honest counsel — even when uncomfortable
The most valuable output of real accountability is honest counsel — advice that reflects the advisor's genuine assessment of the situation, not the advisor's assessment of what the client wants to hear. A client who consistently rewards comfortable counsel and penalizes uncomfortable counsel — even implicitly — will transform an outcome-accountable advisor into a process-accountable one, regardless of the formal compensation structure.
Accountability at Cretta — how the structure manifests in practice

Every engagement Cretta accepts is structured as a mandate — with an explicit result, variable compensation linked to that result, delegated executive authority within the mandate's scope, full information access, and a commitment to continuity through difficulty. This is not a claim — it is a structural description that can be verified in the engagement agreement before the work begins.

The implication for client selection is intentional: Cretta engages with fewer clients and at a higher level of relationship depth than a process-accountable advisory model would allow. Not every client relationship has the bilateral trust, the information access, and the tolerance for process that genuine outcome accountability requires. Cretta does not try to build that trust during the engagement — it is a prerequisite for taking the mandate.

The result for clients who meet those conditions is a fundamentally different advisory experience: one where the entity they have engaged has exactly the same interest in the outcome as they do — and where that alignment is structural, not aspirational.

The standard worth holding — for both sides

Accountability in strategic mandates is easy to claim and difficult to structure. The difficulty is real — outcome accountability in high-complexity situations requires elements from both the advisor and the client that are more demanding than process accountability asks of either party. That demanding standard is not a barrier — it is the feature. It is the mechanism that produces the quality of strategic engagement that genuinely consequential decisions require.

The due diligence for selecting a strategic leadership partner should include, as a first filter, a simple question: what does this entity stand to lose if the result is not produced? If the answer is "nothing beyond the relationship," the accountability is claimed. If the answer involves a specific financial consequence and a demonstrable history of maintaining commitments through difficult processes, the accountability may be real.

In the decisions that most determine the trajectory of a company — transactions, restructurings, pivots, capital events — the difference between claimed and real accountability is not a philosophical distinction. It is the difference between an advisor who will stay when it gets hard and one who will find a way to declare their work done and move on.
