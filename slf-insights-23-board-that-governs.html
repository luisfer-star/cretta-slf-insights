How to Build a Board of Directors
That Actually Governs
(Not Just Supervises)

Most companies with a Board think they have governance. What they have is a meeting — with the right attendees, the right frequency, and the right formality. Governance is something that requires deliberate design — and most Boards were never designed.

By Luis Fernando Jiménez M.
Category Corporate Governance
Read ~12 min

There is a distinction that separates companies with governance from companies with governance theater — and it has nothing to do with the credentials of the Board members, the frequency of sessions, or the thickness of the Board packages. It has to do with whether the Board has ever changed a decision that management would have made without it. If the honest answer is no — or if it requires going back more than two or three sessions to find an example — the Board is not governing. It is supervising. And supervision, however well-intentioned, is not a substitute for governance.

I — The Governing Test

How to know whether your Board actually governs

Before building a Board that governs, it is necessary to assess with honesty whether the current Board does. This assessment does not require a formal evaluation process. It requires honest answers to six specific questions — questions that directly probe the quality of governance, not its form.

The Governance Quality Assessment — Six Questions
01
In the last four Board sessions, did the Board modify any management proposal in a substantive way — not in a detail, but in its core structure or direction? If no, the Board is not deliberating.
02
Has the Board ever stopped a management initiative that it considered misaligned with the company's strategic direction or risk profile? If never, the Board is not governing — it is approving.
03
Does the Board have its own strategic agenda — topics it wants to examine over the next 12 months — independent of what management chooses to present? If no, management controls the governance agenda.
04
Do Board members receive the information package at least seven days before the session, with sufficient time for substantive preparation? If no, the Board cannot deliberate — it can only react.
05
Does the Board have regular private sessions — without management present — to develop its independent perspective? If no, the Board's independence is formal, not functional.
06
Has the Board held management accountable for a commitment that was not met — with a genuine consequence, not a reframing? If no, the accountability system is decorative.
If four or more of these questions produce a "no" or a qualified "yes," the Board requires structural redesign — not a communication workshop.

The Board that has never stopped anything is not a Board that agrees with management on everything. It is a Board that was never given the architecture it needs to disagree effectively — and that has learned, over time, to approve.

II — The Six Components

What a Board that governs is actually built from

A governing Board is not a collection of experienced people who meet regularly. It is an architecture — a set of six interlocking components that must be designed, not assumed. Each component is necessary; none is sufficient alone.

Component 01
The right composition for this company, at this stage
Board composition must be evaluated against the strategic needs of the company in its current stage — not against the preferences of the majority shareholder or the credentials that sound impressive in a bio. A Board built for the company of five years ago is not built for the company of today.
Could each director articulate the company's three most important strategic risks right now?
Component 02
A decision-rights architecture that is explicit and enforced
A governing Board knows precisely which decisions require its approval and which belong to the CEO. Without an explicit decision-rights matrix, the Board either approves everything (rubber-stamping) or intervenes in everything (micro-management). Both destroy governance quality.
Does every Board member know what they can approve without a session and what requires one?
Central element
Strategic information designed by the Board, not management
The Board's information architecture must be defined by the Board itself — what it needs to govern, not what management wants it to see. This is the most frequently missing element in non-governing Boards, and the one with the highest leverage on governance quality.
Did the Board design its own information package — or does it receive what management sends?
Component 04
An agenda that is controlled by governance, not management
The Board's session agenda must be set by the Board Chair in dialogue with the CEO — but with the Board's own governance calendar as the primary driver. A Board whose agenda is set by the CEO is reviewing what management wants reviewed, not governing what governance requires.
Does the Board have a 12-month strategic governance calendar it owns independently?
Component 05
A culture of genuine deliberation — where dissent is expected
The most important cultural norm in a governing Board is that disagreement is not disruptive — it is the mechanism of governance. A Board where directors express reservations privately and validate publicly does not deliberate. It performs deliberation. Building a culture where dissent is structurally safe requires the active leadership of the Board Chair.
Has any director publicly challenged a management proposal in a session this year?
Component 06
A real accountability system with real consequences
Governance without accountability is governance theater. The Board must track commitments made by management across sessions, evaluate their fulfillment against agreed criteria, and have the willingness to act when those commitments are consistently unmet. The accountability system is the most politically difficult component — and the most important.
What was the last commitment the Board held management to that had a real consequence?
III — Board Composition

How to build the right composition — and the profiles that are consistently missing

Board composition is the most consequential design decision in building a governing Board — and the one most frequently made on the wrong criteria. The common approach is to seek directors with impressive credentials and institutional relationships. The correct approach is to identify the specific strategic questions the company will face over the next three to five years and recruit directors who can engage with those questions at the required depth.

IV — The Build Sequence

How to build or rebuild a governing Board — in the right order

Building a governing Board is not a one-time event — it is a phased process that requires sequencing decisions in the right order. Getting the sequence wrong is one of the most common reasons governance redesign efforts fail: the right decisions made in the wrong order produce the wrong outcome.

Phase I — Before any structural decisions
Define what the Board is governing toward
The first question in building a governing Board is not "who should be on the Board?" — it is "what does this Board need to govern, over what horizon, with what accountability structure?" The answer to that question determines the composition, the decision-rights architecture, and the information system. Starting with composition before answering this question produces a Board assembled around credentials rather than governance requirements. The composition follows the mandate — the mandate does not follow the composition.
Phase II — Structural design
Design the decision-rights architecture and information system
Before recruiting directors, design the governance architecture they will inhabit: the decision-rights matrix that defines Board versus CEO authority, the information system that gives the Board what it needs to govern (not what management wants it to see), and the accountability mechanisms that will track management commitments across sessions. Directors recruited into a well-designed governance architecture govern well. Directors recruited into a poorly designed architecture govern poorly — regardless of their individual capability.
Phase III — Composition
Recruit for the gaps the governance mandate requires
With the governance mandate and architecture defined, the composition question becomes specific: which profiles does the Board need to fulfill this governance mandate, at this company, in this stage? Audit the current composition against those requirements. Identify the gaps. Recruit specifically to close them — not to add prestige or relationships that do not directly strengthen the governance function. Every seat on a governing Board has a specific governance purpose. If you cannot articulate the purpose of a seat, the seat is probably decorative.
Phase IV — Culture and practice
Build the deliberation culture through practice, not declaration
Governance culture is not built through a governance charter or a code of conduct — it is built through repeated practice in the session room. The Board Chair who models genuine deliberation — who encourages dissent, who asks the uncomfortable question, who holds management accountable in real time — builds the culture over sessions, not over declarations. The first time the Board exercises genuine governance authority — stops something, modifies something significantly, demands accountability with real consequences — is the moment the culture begins to consolidate.
V — The Common Mistakes

What not to do when building a governing Board

The path toward a governing Board is as much about what to avoid as what to build. The following six patterns appear consistently in governance redesign efforts that produce form without function — Boards that look governing but do not govern.

Mistake 01
Recruiting for prestige instead of purpose
Directors recruited because of their name recognition, their institutional affiliations, or their network value — rather than their specific governance contribution to this company at this stage — almost always underdeliver on governance quality. Prestige and governance effectiveness are frequently uncorrelated.
Mistake 02
Letting management design the Board's information
When the CEO or CFO designs the Board package — deciding what to present, at what level of detail, in what framing — the Board's information is filtered through the very perspective it is supposed to be supervising independently. The Board must own its information architecture.
Mistake 03
Filling the session with reporting instead of deliberation
A session where 80% of the time is spent reviewing what management has done is a reporting session — not a governance session. Governance requires time for deliberation about what should be done next. The reporting function should be pre-read material, not the primary content of the session itself.
Mistake 04
Treating the governance redesign as a one-time project
Building a governing Board is not a project with a completion date — it is an ongoing function that requires continuous attention. The Board's governance needs evolve as the company evolves. A governance architecture designed for the company of today will need redesign as the company grows, enters new markets, or faces new categories of strategic challenge.
Mistake 05
Confusing independence with capability
Independent directors — directors with no management role or significant shareholder stake — are a structural requirement for governance integrity. But independence is not a substitute for competence. An independent director who does not understand the business well enough to challenge management intelligently provides independence without governance value.
Mistake 06
Expecting the Board to self-govern its governance quality
Boards rarely improve their own governance quality through self-reflection alone. The same cultural dynamics that prevent genuine deliberation in sessions also prevent genuine self-assessment in Board evaluations. An external perspective — held by an actor with the mandate and the institutional independence to be honest about what the Board is and is not doing — is almost always necessary for meaningful governance improvement.

The Board the company deserves — built with intention

Every company with a Board thinks it has governance. The discipline of asking — honestly, with evidence — whether the Board has actually changed anything that management would have done without it, is the discipline that separates companies with governance from companies with governance theater. And that discipline is uncomfortable, because the honest answer is frequently "no."

The companies that build governing Boards are not the ones that hire the most impressive directors or produce the most elaborate governance documents. They are the ones that treat governance as an architecture to be designed — with the same intentionality they bring to their business model, their capital structure, and their talent strategy. They design the composition for the mandate, the information system for the Board's needs, the decision-rights matrix for the company's specific context, and the culture for genuine deliberation.

That design does not happen by accident. It requires an actor who understands what governing Boards look like from the inside — and who has the mandate to produce one, not just describe one.
